PSCI-2300: Data and Politics I: Computation and Visualization

Prof. Josh Clinton

Vanderbilt University · Fall 2026

Mondays & Wednesdays · 2:30–3:45 p.m. · Commons 335

AXLE: Social and Behavioral Sciences · CORE: LB–Inquiry Lab · Eligible for Public Policy Studies

How can we use polls to predict election outcomes? What is the impact of minimum wage laws on employment? How can we detect evidence of discrimination? When does a correlation indicate causation? This course will teach you how to address these and other social science questions through data analysis. The course will introduce basic principles of statistical inference and programming skills for data analysis with the goal of providing you with the foundation necessary to analyze data in your own research and to also become more informed and critical consumers of statistical claims made in the news media, in policy reports, and in academic research.

I do not assume you know anything, but I do assume that you are curious, capable, and self-driven as you are a Commodore.

I love this stuff. In part because I think what we are going to learn about is terribly important, perhaps moreso now than every before. Fundamentally, this class about making and evaluating arguments. It is a class about how, not what, to think about the world and it is a class that I hope will both empower you and also transform how you see and understand the world.

A central thesis of this class is that to use data is to make an argument about the world – but an argument that follows some strict rules about the form that the argument takes and also uses language that contains meaning (e.g., correlation, regression). Data is often used to support different sides of an argument about the world, this class aspired to help you better interpret, evaluate, and make data-based claims.

This class is not about math, statistics, or programming; it is about developing critical thinking skills and empowering you with a set of skills to navigate our data-rich reality. I see data science as an endeavor that is based on thinking and creativity – identifying puzzles, questioning claims, figuring out how to answer questions with non-obvious answers. It is also an ongoing quest that demands intellectual humility. While it would be hard to characterize Feynman as humble, his point about the epistemic humility that is required to be truly scientific is worth noting as it is a perspective that I hope this course take seriously.

The scientist has a lot of experience with ignorance and doubt and uncertainty, and this experience is of very great importance, I think. When a scientist doesn’t know the answer to a problem, he is ignorant. When he has a hunch as to what the result is, he is uncertain. And when he is pretty darn sure of what the result is going to be, he is in some doubt. Scientific knowledge is a body of statements of varying degrees of certainty – some most unsure, some nearly sure, none absolutely certain.

— Richard P. Feynman, “The Value of Science” (1955)1

Richard Feynman, circa 1960.2

Who Should and Should Not Take This Course

PSCI 2300 is an introductory statistics and data analysis course, and I encourage students who take this course to continue using statistics. In today’s information-overloaded world, data are available everywhere and the role of statistics is rapidly increasing in academia, business, medicine, public policy, and many other parts of society. I strongly believe that it is an essential skill that everyone benefits from. To quote a headline from The New York Times: “For Today’s Graduate, Just One Word: Statistics.”

Here is a checklist to consider when deciding whether to take this class:

  • I am a political science major or someone who is interested in quantitative social science.
  • In addition to developing my knowledge of statistical concepts, I want to learn the computational skills needed to manipulate and analyze data using the open-source statistical language R.
  • I am willing to spend time outside of classroom each week to keep up with the course materials.
  • I would like to use statistics in a paper and/or job in the future.
  • I understand that this is an introductory class so we will focus primarily, but not exclusively, on developing a conceptual understanding. We will not dive too deep into the mathematical foundations or advanced computational models and methods.

Logistics

Sessions:
MW from 2:30 PM to 3:45 PM in Commons 335

Sessions will be interactive and vary over the course of the semester. The modal class will consist of me introducing concepts and code motivated by real-world questions and data and running the analyses in real-time with you running the same analyses in class. Every class will also have portions that will prompt you to write new code snippets building off of what we are covering to make sure you are tracking with what we are doing. I strongly believe in the importance of being question-driven, not data driven, in your analyses (for reasons that will become clear.). As a result, we will explore data science concepts motivated around trying to answer “real world” questions using actual, real data.

All session notes/code – and any data we will be using – will be posted in the appropriate folder prior to each class on the course website. Make sure you download all of this prior to class and place in the relevant folder. (If I must make last minute changes that require you to download new files in class, I will let you know.)

We meet 28 times. I am away the week of the midterm election because I have to project the 2026 midterm elections for NBC News and away Dec 2 because Vanderbilt has me giving a talk in Palm Beach on prediction markets. The TA will administer Midterm 2 in class on Nov 2 and oversee the working meeting on Dec 2; there is no class Nov 4 (you’re welcome!).

Textbook: None. We will use notes I circulate based on content that either I or my various collaborators have created. There are some free O’Reilly books available via the Vanderbilt Library that may also be useful and which cover similar material. In general, they are more code-driven than question driven. Perhaps the closest to this class – which isn’t very close! – is R for Data Science by Wickham, Çetinkaya-Rundel, and Grolemund: https://r4ds.hadley.nz/

Software: Yes please! Everything we use is freely available. I will circulate information on how to get everything installed if needed.
We will program using the open-source statistical software R: http://www.r-project.org.
We will interact with R using the IDE (Interactive Design Environment) called RStudio: https://posit.co/downloads/
We will create documents and code using Rmarkdown: https://rmarkdown.rstudio.com/

What you will learn

This course carries Vanderbilt’s CORE Inquiry Lab (LB) designation. Inquiry Lab courses teach students how rigorous research is designed, conducted, analyzed, and communicated. Through the class survey project and the other analyses in this course, you will:

  1. formulate research questions and design and execute studies to investigate them;
  2. analyze quantitative data using appropriate methods, interpret the results in context, and diagnose problems in data collection and analysis;
  3. visualize results and communicate research findings effectively in writing and oral presentations; and
  4. practice ethical research and work effectively with others.

The course also satisfies the AXLE Social and Behavioral Sciences requirement for students governed by AXLE and may satisfy part of the Methods of Public Policy requirement for the Public Policy Studies major.

By the end of this course you will be able to:

  1. Load, clean, and combine real data in R — and recognize the operations where data manipulation silently goes wrong.
  2. Describe and visualize data to answer a specific question, honestly.
  3. Quantify uncertainty using resampling: confidence intervals, margins of error, and what they do and do not mean.
  4. Reason about causal claims — what experiments can show, what observational data can and cannot support, and how to tell the difference.
  5. Build and evaluate simple predictive models — regression, classification, and clustering — and know when they are being oversold.
  6. Verify analyses you did not write yourself — including output produced by AI tools. Modern tools will happily produce code and conclusions for you; this course teaches you to be the person in the room who can tell whether they are right.

AI Policy

No AI through Session 6. Do not use AI before Session 7. Never ever use AI for answering in-class questions. We will decide and discuss how to use AI on problem sets in Session 7 and we will actively use AI when analyzing the survey we field.

The rules for quizzes and exams are fixed: they are completed on paper, in class, closed-book, and without devices or AI.

Notes on Problem Sets

For problem sets you are required to submit an electronic copy of the Rmarkdown file and compiled output to Brightspace using xxx.Rmd and xxx.pdf respectively as file names where xxx is your VUID. Both must be submitted for credit!

If you worked with others on the problem set, note that on your submitted problem set. If you use AI (once permitted) note that as well (model-type).

In my opinion, problem sets present an opportunity for you to collaborate and work together. At the same time, there is a clear distinction between permissible collaboration and unacceptable plagiarism. Programming necessitates that you reach your own understanding of the problem and discover a path to its solution – talking things through can help you identify a reasonable solution and help you correct errors in your code. During this time, collaborations are permitted and encouraged. When it comes time to write the final code and analyses, however, such discussions are no longer appropriate: the code and writing must be your own. The same goes with AI. Once we discuss it in class you can use it to help figure out possible coding errors in the ways that we discuss, but never simply ask it to give you the answer – you are all better than that.

Do not, under any circumstances, simply copy and paste another person’s code. Incorporating someone else’s code into your program in any form is a violation of academic regulations. Abetting plagiarism or unauthorized collaboration by sharing your code is also prohibited. Sharing code in digital form is an especially egregious violation: do not e-mail your code to anyone. Novices often have the misconception that copying and mechanically transforming a program (by rearranging independent code, renaming variables, or similar operations) makes it something different.

If you have any questions about these matters, please ask or consult the VU Honor System guidelines: https://www.vanderbilt.edu/student_handbook/the-honor-system/#statement-of-the-honor-code >

Course norms

Politics is the subject of this course, and Fall 2026 is an election season. You are welcome to your political commitments — and this classroom evaluates analysis, not conclusions. The standard for every claim does not depend on the conclusion, but the method and process: what are the data, what was done, and does the evidence support it?

For session dates, topics, assessments, and course materials, see the Schedule.
